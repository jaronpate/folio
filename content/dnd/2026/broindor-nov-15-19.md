There are certain dangers for which every adventurer ought to prepare before visiting a foreign city: cutpurses, assassins, unscrupulous merchants, and the occasional corrupt magistrate.

But few think to prepare for an encounter with a particularly dedicated civil servant.

Nearly three weeks had passed since the company returned from the burial mound of Yarbon. Autumn was surrendering to winter, purses were growing lighter, and the comforts of civilization had proven considerably more expensive than the dangers of the wilderness.

It was then that an old acquaintance offered the company an opportunity to earn an honest wage.

Honesty, as it turned out, would be the least enduring feature of their journey.

## Part I: The Long Road South

On the morning of November the fifteenth, beneath a miserable grey sky, the company assembled outside the gates of Fornost.

Hibernon, the wealthy and exceedingly well-fed merchant whom the adventurers had aided in their earliest travels, was preparing his caravan for the journey south to Ankh-Morpork.

Clad in an expensive fur coat, the great bearded merchant presided over an impressive procession of nearly two hundred souls. There were guards, cooks, drovers, laborers, and wagons laden with goods destined for the southern markets.

Among his passengers was Leo Lannister, nephew to the ruler of Ankh-Morpork.

The young nobleman possessed two remarkable qualities: a voice of extraordinary unpleasantness and an apparently inexhaustible supply of things to complain about.

The company had been hired to serve as his personal escort.

Sigurd rode alongside his loyal companion Karl. Theophrastus, the industrious gnomish thief, joined them, as did Zebloff, a dwarven assassin whose patience would soon prove to be a matter of considerable importance.

There was also Tilde, a nimble wood elf accompanied by his newly employed henchman, Lenny. The cost of retaining another man had placed considerable strain upon the elf's finances, making Hibernon's generous wages particularly welcome.

Rounding out the company was a druid, traveling with his assortment of animal companions. His hawk circled high above the caravan, watching the surrounding countryside as the procession wound its way south.

The rain fell steadily.

Beyond Fornost, they followed the muddy banks of Lake Spinoth before reaching the river that flowed from its waters. There the caravan stopped to feed its passengers and water its animals.

Leo complained.

He complained about the weather, the road, the mud, and the general indignity of traveling through a countryside that seemed unwilling to accommodate him.

Zebloff listened.

And while the others occupied themselves with their midday meal, the dwarf quietly loosened the fastenings beneath Leo's saddle.

When the caravan resumed its journey, Leo's horse trotted forward. Its saddle shifted, and the young nobleman disappeared unceremoniously into the mud.

Fortunately for both Leo and the company's wages, he suffered no serious injury.

Sigurd hurried to assist him. Zebloff, with remarkable composure, helped the young man regain his seat.

Leo regarded the dwarf with newfound appreciation.

“You know, for a dwarf, you're not so bad.”

Rarely had a compliment been bestowed upon a less deserving recipient.

The following days passed without further incident. The caravan traveled through Fern Gully, crossed the boundaries of Lord Guthrie's domain, and entered the County of Lannister.

They traversed broad grasslands, scrub-covered plains, and cultivated fields, making camp each evening beneath the cold November sky.

The druid and his beasts kept watch. Sigurd and Karl took their turns guarding the camp. Even Zebloff and Tilde eventually lent their services to the nightly vigil.

No bandits came. No monsters emerged from the darkness. No great calamity befell the caravan.

Indeed, the journey was so uneventful that one might almost have mistaken the company for competent professionals.

By the afternoon of November the eighteenth, the walls of Ankh-Morpork rose before them.

## Part II: A Matter of Administration

Ankh-Morpork was an ancient and sprawling city surrounded by farmland, with the ocean lying beyond its southeastern reaches.

According to local legend, Ankh and Morpork had once been two separate cities, divided by a river. Some forgotten magical catastrophe had caused the river to vanish, leaving behind a single great settlement whose inhabitants remembered considerably more about commerce than they did about their own history.

The first lesson the adventurers learned about Ankh-Morpork was that entering it cost a gold piece.

Even escorting a member of House Lannister afforded them no exemption.

Hibernon's quartermaster distributed their wages for the four days of travel, and the company proceeded toward the palace with Leo.

They had not gone far when they were intercepted by a city official named Theodore.

Theodore was accompanied by a formidable-looking guard and possessed the grave expression of a man who had discovered an irregularity in the natural order of things.

Zebloff, he explained, was not wearing the mandatory golden hammer upon his shoulder.

All dwarves within Ankh-Morpork were required to register with the authorities and display this identifying emblem at all times.

The penalty for failing to do so was ten gold pieces.

Theophrastus, wishing to avoid any similar misunderstanding, politely inquired whether gnomes were subject to comparable regulations.

Theodore informed him that he was most certainly a dwarf.

Sigurd attempted to explain the misunderstanding, but the official was possessed of that peculiar confidence found in men whose authority greatly exceeds their imagination.

Zebloff agreed to pay the fine.

Unfortunately, Theodore was not yet finished.

For twenty minutes, the company stood in the street while the official painstakingly prepared a written citation. When Zebloff finally offered him the money, Theodore refused it.

“You're not supposed to pay me. Take the citation to the office by the docks. You'll be issued your identification there.”

The dwarf asked for directions.

“I haven't time to escort dwarves around the city,” Theodore replied. “I'm sure you're capable of finding it.”

There are moments in history when the fate of a man hinges upon a single ill-considered sentence.

This was such a moment.

Zebloff drew his bastard sword.

Before Theodore or his guard could react, the blade swept through the air. :margin-figure{src="/dnd/2026/broindor/nov-15-19/zebloff_cuts_down_a_racist.png" alt="Zebloff, a braided dwarf in dark armor, cuts down a city official in a wet Ankh-Morpork street as papers scatter and a guard recoils." caption="Zebloff cuts down a racist."} The official's head departed his shoulders.

For a brief moment, there was silence.

Sigurd moved Leo away from the scene and turned toward Theodore's guard.

“Just walk away,” he advised. “We're taking Leo to the palace. Find yourself another employment.”

The guard looked at the corpse. He looked at the adventurers. He studied their faces carefully.

Then he fled.

Tilde quietly instructed Lenny to follow him.

Leo stared in horror at Zebloff.

“I thought you were cool!”

Meanwhile, Theophrastus helped dispose of Theodore's body while the druid permitted his hungry badger to make off with several of the unfortunate man's fingers.

Sigurd could only shake his head.

The company had been inside Ankh-Morpork for less than an afternoon.

Already, its population had diminished by one.

## Part III: The Lion and the Living Hook

Despite the unfortunate interruption, the company reached the palace before news of Theodore's demise appeared to have reached its gates.

Leo was recognized by the palace guards and invited his escorts inside.

There they were brought before Tywin Lannister, Count of Ankh-Morpork and a powerful nobleman within the greater Kingdom of Hart.

An interesting expression crossed Tywin's face when he saw his nephew.

For the briefest instant, he appeared disappointed.

Then he rose warmly.

“Leo! I'm so glad you've made it safely. We've missed you.”

Leo confirmed the company's account of his rescue from the bandits and his subsequent journey home.

Tywin, wishing to demonstrate his gratitude, offered a reward.

It was at this moment that Sigurd produced a sealed letter from Lord Guthrie.

The count's expression soured considerably.

“Well,” he said, accepting the letter, “I suppose I shall still reward you, even if you've brought me correspondence from that absolute barbarian in the north.”

Each adventurer received five platinum pieces, together with an invitation to reside within the palace for the remainder of November. Food, lodging, and entertainment would be provided at the count's expense.

For a company whose finances had recently become a source of concern, this was a most agreeable arrangement.

Theophrastus then requested something rather more unusual than gold.

A letter confirming that he was not a dwarf.

This brought Tywin's attention to Zebloff's conspicuous lack of identification.

“You should acquire one of those hammers,” he advised. “Otherwise, my bureaucrats will fine you. And I strongly recommend against interfering with them. The punishments are quite severe.”

The company listened with considerable interest.

Tywin explained that the measures had originated following a war with the dwarven kingdom of the northwest some ten or fifteen years earlier. Originally, the authorities had merely required dwarven residents to register. Over time, the bureaucracy had transformed this requirement into a mandatory public display of their identity.

Dwarves were permitted to live throughout the city, though prejudice had driven most of them into a district of their own.

The registration itself cost a mere ten copper pieces.

Neglecting to answer a citation, however, could result in a month's imprisonment or a fine of five hundred gold pieces.

None of this appeared especially promising for Zebloff.

Sigurd wisely steered the conversation toward opportunities for employment.

The count's lands were well patrolled, and he had little trouble with bandits. Within the city itself, however, another power had begun to challenge his authority.

The Thieves' Guild.

Tywin had maintained an understanding with its leader for some time. Recently, that understanding had deteriorated.

Pirates were attacking his ships at sea, stealing their cargo, and selling the plunder back through the criminal markets of his own city.

“It costs me money,” Tywin explained, “but more importantly, it's embarrassing.”

Sending soldiers against the guild threatened to embroil half the city in bloodshed. He preferred a quieter solution.

“If somebody could bring me his head, things would be considerably simpler.”

Tilde asked the name of the man they sought.

“Benny Hookhand.”

A fitting name for a criminal of the docks, certainly.

Except that Benny Hookhand was not a man.

He was the hook.

The unremarkable steel contraption possessed an intelligence of its own and the power to seize control of those who took possession of it. Its unfortunate bearers became puppets through which Benny conducted his affairs.

Killing the bearer would accomplish little. The hook itself had to be recovered.

“And precisely how does one become possessed?” Tilde asked. “By wearing it? Or merely touching it?”

Tywin shook his head.

“For obvious reasons, Benny Hookhand has never explained the finer workings of his magic to me.”

The guild reportedly numbered a hundred members or more, though Benny maintained a smaller circle of close associates. Its headquarters changed frequently, but much of its activity centered upon the docks.

Here was a challenge worthy of the company's attention.

Or, at the very least, an opportunity to commit crimes with a somewhat clearer purpose.

## Part IV: The Misfortunes of Rudolph Rednose

Before leaving the palace, Zebloff obtained his golden hammer. Theophrastus was issued one as well, his objections regarding his ancestry having apparently achieved nothing.

Outside, Lenny returned from his pursuit of Theodore's guard.

He whispered his findings privately to Tilde, who kept the contents of the report to himself.

With that, the company proceeded toward the docks.

They had scarcely begun their investigation when a lanky man clad in leather came hurtling from the doorway of a tavern and collided with Theophrastus.

Both fell to the ground.

Sigurd and Tilde helped them to their feet.

The stranger introduced himself as Rudolph.

“Sorry,” he told the gnome. “I don't usually look down that low.”

He then expressed his dissatisfaction with the tavern's ale and announced that he knew a superior establishment nearby.

The company followed.

Upon discovering that dwarves were among his new acquaintances, Rudolph displayed considerably less enthusiasm for their companionship.

He then vomited upon a dwarf's footwear.

Having relieved himself of this burden, Rudolph straightened with renewed vigor.

“Oh, that feels much better.”

And off he went in the opposite direction.

An attempt to stop him earned another insult and a kick, at which point Tilde and Lenny decided that the ranger had exhausted his supply of goodwill.

Now, Tilde stood scarcely four feet and seven inches tall and weighed less than ninety pounds. He was not a creature naturally suited to feats of overwhelming physical strength.

Nevertheless, agility had its virtues.

With a well-placed strike and a sudden grapple, the little elf brought Rudolph under control. The others surrounded him.

The ranger found himself answering questions.

His full name, he revealed, was Rudolph Rednose.

As for Benny Hookhand, he insisted that he knew nothing of his whereabouts.

“I'm a ranger! I like the forest, and when I'm in the city, I like to drink. That's all!”

Zebloff suggested removing one of his legs.

Rudolph quickly remembered something.

There was a tavern near the docks known as the Left Clog. A foreign merchant named Raku frequented the establishment, offering substances considerably more potent than ale.

Raku often disappeared into the neighboring alleys, and Rudolph suspected that some of his customers were involved in less respectable enterprises.

It was not much, but it was a beginning.

Before releasing the ranger, Tilde relieved him of a purse containing twenty gold pieces.

“Drinks are on me, boys.”

Sigurd was becoming increasingly concerned with the company's methods.

“Can we stop murdering and robbing people?”

“We've only killed one person,” Tilde observed.

This was true.

It was also a rather poor defense.

## Part V: The Left Clog

Nestled between two establishments whose occupants claimed to be seamstresses, the Left Clog was precisely the sort of tavern in which respectable travelers were unlikely to find themselves by accident.

By evening, the company had settled inside.

Sigurd, Karl, and the druid occupied themselves with ale, while Tilde and Zebloff approached Raku when the man appeared.

The merchant was clearly from distant lands, and he wasted little time discussing his merchandise.

For twenty gold pieces, he offered a tiny glass vial containing several drops of blue liquid.

A potion, he claimed, capable of making an evening with the neighboring seamstresses considerably more enjoyable.

Tilde purchased one.

Thus did Rudolph Rednose's twenty gold pieces pass into the economy of Ankh-Morpork, having barely warmed the elf's pocket.

Zebloff inquired how many additional potions Raku carried and whether he had associates nearby.

The merchant grew suspicious.

When Tilde finally mentioned Benny Hookhand, Raku became decidedly uncomfortable.

“Who said anything about the hook? What are you, some kind of city guard?”

The elf explained that they were merely conducting business and hoped to acquire a contact through whom certain valuable goods might be sold.

Raku was unconvinced.

“Look, I don't know you. But if you keep asking questions like that, you're probably going to receive a visit from the hook.”

To the company, this sounded almost encouraging.

Raku departed shortly thereafter, leaving them with little more than a blue potion and a warning.

Across the tavern, however, Sigurd had uncovered a different mystery.

The barkeep told him that sailors occasionally spoke of a great spire rising from the ocean beyond the coast.

It appeared only upon the darkest nights, when clouds concealed the stars and even the moon afforded little illumination.

For twenty years, the barkeep had dismissed these stories as the usual inventions of men who spent too long at sea.

But a new rumor had given the old tale fresh life.

Some two weeks earlier, a man had supposedly been shipwrecked upon the mysterious spire and returned – pockets heavy with treasure.

How he had escaped, or even reached its summit, nobody seemed to know.

Nor could anyone say precisely where the spire stood.

For it was not always there.

## Part VI: Three Witnesses with Feathers

As the evening grew late, the druid turned to an avenue of investigation unavailable to his companions.

The animals of the city.

Rats scurried beneath the tables, but the distinction between a man carrying a knife and a man possessing a metal hand was evidently too subtle a matter for their understanding.

The druid consequently entrusted his inquiries to three seagulls.

Through the ancient magic of his calling, he opened a brief communion with the birds and explained the peculiar nature of the man they sought.

The gulls squawked among themselves.

To his surprise, they reported having seen not one, but two individuals with hooks in place of hands.

One had been observed wandering the streets near the docks, though the birds could not recall precisely where.

The other had been seen aboard a small vessel.

Even after the enchantment faded, one of the gulls led the company toward the waterfront, where it settled upon a little skiff moored beside a much larger sailing ship.

The boat was empty.

While the others kept watch, Tilde climbed aboard and searched its interior. He discovered a canteen of water, a coil of rope, and little else.

The druid dispatched his hawk to circle the neighboring two-masted vessel. The bird reported the presence of three men upon its deck.

With no evidence connecting the larger ship to Benny Hookhand, the companions wisely refrained from boarding it.

For once, they allowed an innocent vessel to remain innocent.

They returned to the palace without further trouble and retired beneath the silken sheets provided by their generous host.

The first night in Ankh-Morpork had come to an end.

Somewhere in the city, a city guard knew their faces, a ranger was missing twenty gold pieces, and the widow or relatives of a certain bureaucrat had yet to learn why Theodore would not be returning home.

## Part VII: The Great Northern Evil

On the morning of November the nineteenth, the company divided its efforts.

Sigurd and Tilde made their way to the city's grand academy, while the druid, Theophrastus, and Zebloff returned to the docks.

Sigurd carried with him a rare volume recovered during an earlier adventure: the holy texts of Zethir.

He sought a scholarly friar named Tom Aquilla, whose interest in such writings had previously been brought to his attention.

They found the learned man delivering a lecture upon the second chapter of the Book of John.

Three hours had passed, during which the friar had successfully examined four sentences.

He was evidently a man who regarded brevity as an intellectual failing.

When the lecture concluded, Sigurd presented the holy texts.

Tom's eyes widened.

“Is that genuinely Zethir's holy texts? I've been searching for a copy for years!”

There were few things in the world, he confessed, that he loved more than books. Although his personal funds were limited, he believed the academy could be persuaded to purchase the volume.

Sigurd named his price.

One thousand gold pieces.

The friar placed a hand upon his heart as though Sigurd had thrust a dagger into it.

“A thousand gold? I've taken a vow of poverty!”

Negotiations followed.

Three hundred gold became five hundred, then four hundred, until the discussion turned toward something Sigurd had discovered within the writings themselves.

A reference to the Great Northern Evil of Ice.

The words intrigued Tom considerably.

Whether they referred to an ancient supernatural entity or merely reflected the author's intense dislike of winter remained uncertain.

Nevertheless, the friar proposed a new arrangement.

He would purchase the book for three hundred and fifty gold pieces, investigate the meaning of the mysterious passage, and undertake one further scholarly inquiry on behalf of the adventurers at a future date.

The offer was accepted.

Tom promised to begin his research immediately.

There are treasures which glitter, and treasures which do not. The company had acquired both that morning, though only time would reveal which was worth more.

## Part VIII: The Captain Without a Ship

Meanwhile, at the docks, the others returned to the little skiff.

This time, it was occupied.

An old seafarer with a hook in place of his hand sat upon one of its benches, eating a loaf of bread and gazing peacefully toward the ocean.

Zebloff called to him.

“Benny!”

The man did not respond.

The dwarf considered this insufficient proof of his innocence.

The druid, adopting a somewhat more conventional approach, greeted him.

The stranger introduced himself as Patchy.

He was an experienced captain who had sailed with a number of crews, some of them not entirely lawful. At present, however, he was unemployed, a fact made abundantly clear by the modest dimensions of his vessel.

The druid offered him a silver coin in exchange for information about Benny Hookhand.

Patchy reached for the coin.

Then he heard the name.

He promptly returned it.

“No, thank you. I'd prefer to stay alive.”

Everybody knew of Benny, he explained. Everybody knew that the hook ruled the city's underworld. And everybody knew that asking too many questions about it might result in becoming its next bearer.

“I've no interest in becoming a hook-handed man.”

“You already are a hook-handed man,” the druid pointed out.

Patchy raised his metal appendage.

“Yes, but I'm in charge of my own body. This is just a piece of metal.”

The druid summoned his badger, which regarded the sailor with growing hostility.

Theophrastus then attempted an ingenious act of intimidation.

He whispered to Zebloff to play along, drew his dagger, and placed it against the dwarf's throat.

“Tell us what you know, or I'll kill this dwarf!”

Patchy looked at him.

Then he looked at Zebloff.

“Why should I care?”

Theophrastus had evidently overestimated the value placed upon dwarven lives in Ankh-Morpork.

Patchy insisted that he knew little of the criminal underworld. But if they required someone who knew the surrounding seas like the back of his hand, he was their man.

He glanced briefly at his hook.

“The other hand, that is.”

Remembering the barkeep's tale, the druid asked him about the mysterious spire.

At this, Patchy's demeanor changed.

## Part IX: The Spire Beyond the Shore

The legend, Patchy explained, was older than Ankh-Morpork itself.

To glimpse the spire, one had to sail far enough into the ocean that the coastline disappeared entirely. Even then, it revealed itself only beneath a sky almost wholly devoid of light.

Patchy had sought it many times throughout his years at sea.

Twice, he had found it.

And on those two occasions, it had stood in entirely different places.

“Once to the north, once to the south. I know these waters. I know where I was.”

He described a colossal structure rising sheer from the surrounding waves, taller than the tallest mast of any ship. Its sides were unnaturally smooth, and the churning sea made approaching it exceedingly dangerous.

On neither occasion had he managed to land.

It appeared almost as though it had been fashioned by deliberate hands, though Patchy could scarcely imagine what manner of creature could have built such a thing.

As for the tale of the shipwrecked man who had returned with treasure, the captain remained skeptical.

“If you crashed against it, you'd sink. There'd be nothing to hold onto. No way to climb.”

Yet the rumor persisted.

And if even part of Patchy's account was true, then somewhere beyond the familiar shores lay a structure which defied the ordinary arrangements of the world.

The druid gave the captain a copper piece for his trouble, and the companions departed.

Patchy retained his hook, his limbs, and his life.

Given the nature of his visitors, he had enjoyed a remarkably fortunate morning.

The company reunited over sandwiches at Jimmy's, where the discoveries of the academy and the docks were exchanged.

There was much to consider.

A living enchantment commanded the criminal underworld. Pirates plundered the count's ships. An ancient text spoke of an evil dwelling in the northern ice. And beyond the reach of the coastline, a mysterious spire rose and vanished beneath the darkest skies.

All the while, the consequences of Zebloff's encounter with Theodore remained unsettled.

For a party that had arrived in Ankh-Morpork scarcely a day earlier, they had accumulated an impressive number of reasons to stay.

And at least one very compelling reason to leave.

Thus ended the company's first adventures in Ankh-Morpork.

They had crossed the countryside without facing a single enemy, yet within the safety of civilization had managed to commit murder, robbery, and several acts of questionable diplomacy.

They had earned respectable wages, received the hospitality of a powerful nobleman, and secured the services of a learned sage.

And still, their greatest discoveries were not things they possessed, but things they had yet to understand.

Somewhere among the crowded alleys, Benny Hookhand carried on his affairs through hands that were not his own.

In the academy, Tom Aquilla began his search through ancient writings for the meaning of a forgotten evil.

And far beyond the harbor, where the light of civilization surrendered to the darkness of the ocean, there waited a spire which no sailor could reliably find and no captain could explain.

The company had come south in pursuit of coin.

They had found it.

But gold, as the wise would tell them, was seldom the most dangerous thing awaiting discovery.

And the nights, after all, were growing longer.

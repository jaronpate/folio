Of all the creatures known to inhabit the world, there are few so difficult to understand as men who make their living dishonestly.

A dragon could generally be found in its lair. A troll might reasonably be expected beneath a bridge. Even the walking dead, dreadful though they were, seldom made any great effort to conceal their occupation.

But the thieves of Ankh-Morpork conducted their affairs behind legitimate businesses, among respectable citizens, and beneath the noses of men who had sworn to uphold the law.

Finding their master would require considerably more than a sharp sword.

Unfortunately, sharp swords were among the things the company understood best.

## Part I: A Most Unusual Dressmaker

On a Sunday morning in late November, the adventurers assembled once more within Ankh-Morpork.

Joining their company was Sledge, a forty-seven-year-old half-elven cleric newly arrived among their ranks. He found himself in the company of Sigurd and his retainers, Karl and Conrad; Tilde and his fellow elf, Lenny; and Misty, a monk accompanied by a human priest named Babelzeru.

The matter of Benny Hookhand remained their principal concern.

Sigurd had spent some time gathering rumors throughout the city, and his inquiries had yielded three establishments worthy of investigation.

The first was Madame Butane's Dresses, a business rumored to maintain connections with the Thieves' Guild. The second was George's General Stuff, where stolen merchandise supposedly found its way back into respectable commerce.

The third was Dock Number Nine, reputed to be a gathering place for smugglers.

Misty expressed her desire to restore order to the city. Tilde remained concerned with how they might approach Benny without immediately making enemies of his associates.

Sledge, having heard the peculiar circumstances surrounding the enchanted hook, offered a refreshingly uncomplicated opinion.

“Let's get him.”

Before departing, the company agreed upon the proper division of any treasure they might acquire. It was a sensible discussion, particularly among people who had yet to earn anything.

Their first destination was the dressmaker.

The plan was to present Misty as a wealthy customer seeking an expensive gown, thereby occupying the proprietor while the more discreet members of the company investigated the premises.

By midmorning, they arrived at an establishment bearing a rather proud sign.

Madame Butane's Dresses.

The doors were closed.

A drunken man lay sleeping outside, and through the windows the company could discern several other occupants who appeared to be enjoying a similarly unproductive morning.

Sigurd nudged the sleeping man with his boot.

“Wake up. What do you know about this shop?”

The fellow opened his eyes, mumbled something unintelligible, rolled over, and vomited.

Sigurd stepped aside.

His previous experiences with the drunkards of Ankh-Morpork had at least taught him something.

Finding their first witness of little assistance, the warrior proceeded to pound upon the door.

“Hold your britches!” came a shrill voice from within.

Several minutes passed before the door creaked open upon its chain. Through the narrow opening appeared the heavily painted eye of an older woman.

“What do you want?”

“We're here to buy a dress,” Sigurd explained. “We've an important occasion.”

The woman stared at him.

Then she began to laugh.

When she attempted to close the door, Sigurd wedged his boot against it.

“What sort of establishment do you think this is?” she demanded.

“A dress shop?”

The woman glanced along the street.

“It's a dress shop when the taxman is asking.”

“Well,” offered Tilde, “we're not the taxman.”

“Then it's not a dress shop.” she quipped.

The nature of the establishment gradually became apparent.

Its occupants were seamstresses in much the same sense that the company's investigation was an innocent shopping excursion.

Tilde produced the small vial of blue liquid he had purchased from Raku several nights earlier.

“Do you know anything about this?”

The woman recognized it immediately.

“Oh, I've seen that. It's made for some good nights and some bad ones.”

She informed them that the establishment opened properly at six in the evening.

With that, the company withdrew, and the door slammed shut behind them.

They had learned nothing of Benny Hookhand, though they had acquired a somewhat better understanding of the city's textile industry.

## Part II: The Dock That Wasn't There

The waterfront of Ankh-Morpork was a sprawling affair, crowded with warehouses, vessels, cargo, and laborers.

The adventurers arrived near a dock bearing the number forty-two and began making their way along the harbor.

Eventually, they reached Dock Number Ten.

Beyond it stood Dock Number Eight.

They stopped.

There was no Dock Number Nine.

Between the two docks, however, five rough-looking fellows had gathered near a collection of crates. Two were dwarves, and the little company appeared entirely occupied with a game of dice.

Tilde approached.

“Who's in charge here?”

The men looked at one another.

“We're playing dice. In charge of what?”

The elf explained that he was a foreigner seeking an introduction and produced a letter of recommendation he had acquired in Fornost.

He handed it to one of the men.

The tallest among them promptly snatched it away.

“This idjit can't read.”

After examining the letter himself, the man regarded Tilde's companions with suspicion.

“This doesn't mention an entire company of do-gooders.”

“They're my men,” Tilde assured him. “You think I work alone?”

The elf explained that he represented interests in Fornost and hoped to establish a profitable arrangement between the two cities. He had goods to move, business to conduct, and little interest in dealing with men who could not introduce him to somebody of consequence.

The stranger remained unconvinced.

Even the name upon the letter was insufficient to establish trust.

Tilde produced his blue potion.

The man recognized it, claiming to know its maker, but was no more impressed by the vial than he had been by the letter.

“Anybody can buy one of those.”

It became apparent that introductions within the criminal fraternity were not freely bestowed.

Fortunately, the stranger had a proposition.

On the opposite side of the city stood an establishment known as Bob's Bits. Among its assorted curiosities was a particular pair of boots, entirely black in color and possessing magical properties of considerable usefulness to men in certain occupations.

If Tilde could acquire those boots and deliver them, the stranger would arrange a meeting with his superior.

The man introduced himself as Joe.

Before departing, Tilde asked one final question.

“Any chance you could tell us where Dock Nine is?”

Joe looked at him as though addressing a particularly troublesome child.

“Who said anything about Dock Nine? There is no Dock Nine. Can't you read the numbers? Ten and eight. Get out of here.”

The company withdrew.

Sigurd was unimpressed by the arrangement.

If Tilde intended to go to the trouble of stealing a pair of enchanted boots, he reasoned, surely the elf ought to keep them for himself rather than surrender them to some fellow playing dice beside a warehouse.

There was a certain logic to this.

Though perhaps not the sort of logic that encouraged lasting commercial relationships.

## Part III: Lady Warnsworth

Leaving the waterfront, the company encountered a young gentlewoman carrying a basket of bread and reading a book.

She had evidently become so absorbed in her reading that she had wandered considerably beyond the respectable districts of Ankh-Morpork.

Sigurd greeted her courteously.

“Good day, my lady. Might you require an escort home? We are adventurers.”

The woman looked up.

Upon examining the armed strangers surrounding her, she immediately turned to flee.

Sigurd, rather wounded in his dignity, made no attempt to pursue her.

It was Misty who succeeded where the warrior had failed.

With a serene expression and a gentler manner, the monk reassured the frightened woman, taking an interest in her book and explaining that despite the somewhat alarming appearance of her companions, she need not fear them.

The lady soon realized that she was nowhere near the part of town she had intended to visit.

She gratefully accepted their assistance.

Along the way, they encountered six leatherworkers returning from their midday meal.

One recognized their charge immediately.

“Lady Warnsworth! Who are these companions of yours?”

She explained that the strangers had kindly offered to escort her home.

The tradesman appeared puzzled by her presence in that neighborhood.

Lady Warnsworth mumbled something about a particularly interesting book.

The fellow chuckled.

Apparently, this was not the first occasion upon which literature had carried her somewhere unintended.

Before the leatherworkers departed, Sigurd decided to make an inquiry.

“Would any of you happen to know anything about Dock Nine?”

Most looked confused.

One did not.

The man, whose companions called him Bill, caught Sigurd's eye and quietly motioned for him to remain silent.

“We've just come from Parker's Pub,” he announced suddenly. “Excellent beer there. I imagine we'll be back this evening.”

His companions looked at him strangely.

Nobody had asked about the quality of the city's beer.

Sigurd understood.

“Then this evening, we too shall drink there.”

By two in the afternoon, Lady Warnsworth had been safely returned to her rather magnificent residence in the northeastern quarter of the city.

She thanked the company warmly.

There was no reward of gold, no promise of employment, and no enchanted heirloom bestowed upon her rescuers.

Misty was perfectly content with this arrangement.

They had performed a good deed, and that was sufficient.

Indeed, kindness had brought them their most promising lead of the day, which was more than could be said for threatening drunkards, displaying suspicious correspondence, or visiting houses of questionable tailoring.

## Part IV: The Price of Fear

There remained several hours before the appointed meeting at Parker's Pub.

The company therefore resumed its journey toward George's General Stuff.

Along the way, they encountered nine brown-robed pilgrims carrying wooden staves and holy symbols. The travelers stood huddled together, arguing over the location of the Shrine of Saint Jerome.

By happy coincidence, the shrine was situated near the very establishment the adventurers sought.

Sigurd invited them to follow.

The pilgrims accepted enthusiastically.

Then they began to sing.

It was a holy song of considerable volume and remarkably little harmony. Some sang, others chanted, and each appeared to possess his own understanding of the melody.

Nevertheless, the procession reached its destination.

The leader thanked the adventurers and offered to bestow a healing blessing upon them should they ever require his services. He had recently become an acolyte as he astutely pointed out.

Many of the party members stared blankly before Sigurd thanked him.

The company continued into George's establishment, leaving the pilgrims to locate their shrine and trouble the heavens with their music.

George's General Stuff appeared to be an ordinary mercantile business.

Its inventory was eclectic, consisting largely of whatever merchandise its proprietor had recently acquired from others.

Among the more remarkable items were two enchanted wands.

One possessed the power to inspire supernatural terror in its victims. The other could reveal hidden doors and concealed traps.

George wanted twenty thousand gold pieces for the former.

For the latter, thirty thousand.

These were sums of money which the adventurers possessed chiefly in their imaginations.

Sigurd nevertheless entertained the possibility of exchanging his Wand of Metal and Mineral Detection for something of broader usefulness.

George examined the prospect.

The warrior's wand possessed ninety-six charges, while the Wand of Fear contained only forty. Yet George maintained that detecting metals was a relatively commonplace enchantment.

He offered to exchange the wands if Sigurd included an additional thousand gold pieces.

No agreement was reached.

Tilde then presented his letter of recommendation.

George's expression changed.

After ensuring that no other customers were within earshot, the merchant admitted that some of his merchandise came from individuals who would be reluctant to explain its origins before a magistrate.

He was, in short, a fence.

“It's not a complicated business,” he explained. “The difficult part is knowing whom to trust.”

The letter persuaded him that Tilde was unlikely to betray him to the authorities. It did not, however, persuade him to arrange an introduction to the Thieves' Guild.

The city guard had been growing troublesome, and George saw little advantage in involving himself in affairs beyond his own shop.

The conversation deteriorated somewhat when the name of Tywin Lannister entered it.

George had no desire whatsoever to discuss the ruler of Ankh-Morpork with strangers.

Nor did he wish to purchase an unidentified magical scroll which Tilde offered him. He suggested that the elf visit the wizards' tower near the palace and have its contents examined before attempting to sell it.

With little more to be gained, the company departed.

George retained his wands.

Sigurd retained his gold.

And Tilde retained a collection of possessions which he seemed increasingly determined to persuade somebody else to take.

## Part V: An Honest Man with a Dishonest Past

As evening approached, the company encountered the leatherworkers once more.

The craftsmen were making their way toward Parker's Pub, and conversation turned naturally toward the adventurers' origins in Fornost and their previous employment under Lord Guthrie.

“What manner of work do you do?” one asked.

“Whatever work needs doing,” answered Sigurd.

“Personnel adjustment,” added Misty.

The tradesman appeared impressed by Sigurd's considerable physique and reached out to examine his arm.

“Watch this,” said the warrior.

He promptly lifted the unfortunate fellow into the air.

The man shrieked.

Sigurd set him down again, patted him awkwardly upon the shoulder, and brought the demonstration to an end.

Having established their professional qualifications, the company returned briefly to the palace to collect Alikai, the druid whose communion with the seagulls had previously aided their investigation.

Together, they reached Parker's Pub at seven in the evening.

The establishment was lively and crowded, filled with working men enjoying the conclusion of their day.

Bill sat in a corner.

Rather than descend upon him as an entire armed company, the adventurers mingled with the patrons while Tilde approached the leatherworker.

Tilde provided ale, and the conversation began.

Bill was initially cautious.

He was an honest leatherworker now, he explained, though there had been a time when his livelihood depended upon less respectable endeavors.

And yes, he knew about Dock Nine.

There had never been a ninth dock. Some error in the numbering had left the space between Docks Eight and Ten without a proper designation, a circumstance which the Thieves' Guild had found sufficiently amusing to adopt as its own.

A small contingent of guild members ordinarily remained there to arrange the purchase and sale of larger quantities of stolen merchandise.

Without knowing the proper password, however, strangers would find them unwilling to conduct genuine business.

Bill also explained something of the rhythm of the waterfront.

During the day, honest cargo occupied the docks.

During the evening, the neighboring taverns and seamstresses attracted crowds of revelers.

But in the hours before dawn, when the laborers had yet to rise and the drunkards had finally exhausted themselves, the waterfront grew quiet.

That was when the smugglers conducted their affairs.

The guards responsible for patrolling the area were often bribed to occupy themselves elsewhere on certain nights. Their absence was carefully arranged so as not to attract the suspicion of their superiors.

Not every exchange involved a ship laden with stolen cargo. Sometimes the missing dock merely served as a meeting place for conducting business.

Bill had abandoned that life more than a year earlier and could not say precisely when the next gathering would occur. But he believed that watching the area during the darkest hours of the morning would eventually yield results.

The company finally possessed something better than rumor.

They had a place to watch and an hour at which to watch it.

And although they discussed recruiting Captain Patchy and observing the waterfront from the water, they ultimately decided upon a simpler arrangement.

They would return to the palace, sleep for several hours, and depart for the docks at two in the morning.

Most of them, anyway.

Tilde had another appointment to keep.

## Part VI: The Tiger in the Alley

While his companions retired to the comforts of the palace, Tilde and Lenny set out for Madame Butane's establishment.

The hour was late, and the two elves made their way through the narrow, crooked alleys of Ankh-Morpork.

It was there that they encountered a solitary woman.

She appeared formidable enough in her ordinary shape, but there was something peculiar about the manner in which she moved through the darkness. She was plainly attempting to avoid notice, and when she realized that the elves had seen her, her expression twisted with fury.

Then her body began to change.

Her limbs lengthened. Fur emerged across her flesh, and the features of her face gave way to those of a great predatory cat.

A weretiger!

Tilde and Lenny did not remain to observe the completion of this remarkable transformation....they ran.

By the time the creature had assumed its monstrous shape, the elves had nearly reached the end of the alley. A terrible roar followed them, and the weretiger bounded into pursuit. :margin-figure{src="/dnd/2026/broindor/nov-22-23/elves_soil_their_pants.jpg" alt="Tilde and Lenny, two sheepish elves, run in terror from a freakishly large tiger." caption="Tilde and Lenny soil their pants." maxWidth="26rem"}

She was gaining.

The passage divided ahead.

Tilde turned sharply to the left and hurled a ceramic jug of oil onto the stones behind him.

It shattered.

Moments later, the weretiger rounded the corner at full speed, struck the spreading oil, and briefly discovered that even the extraordinary agility of a great cat was no guarantee against a slippery pavement.

The elves gained a precious few moments.

They continued through the alley, casting darts behind them as they fled. At the next corner, Tilde collided awkwardly with the wall, allowing the creature to draw closer once more.

Onward they ran until the passage opened into a broader street.

To one side stood two laborers unloading a cart.

Tilde and Lenny rushed toward them, vaulting over a crate and provoking a chorus of angry shouts.

The men demanded to know what the devil they were doing.

The elves glanced behind them.

The weretiger was gone.

Whether the creature feared additional witnesses or had simply lost interest in the pursuit, they could not know. But Tilde was understandably reluctant to investigate.

He offered the laborers some money for the disturbance, which they accepted with considerable grumbling.

After taking a circuitous route through the city, the two elves finally reached ther destination: Madame Butane's Dresses.

It had proven an unusually strenuous journey for an evening of leisure.

## Part VII: The Madam's Business

The establishment bore little resemblance to the shuttered building the company had visited that morning.

Light spilled from its open doors. Music, conversation, and laughter filled the interior, and its many occupants appeared to have little immediate need for dresses.

Behind the counter stood the woman whose painted eye had greeted the company earlier.

Madame Butane herself.

Tilde and Lenny settled inside, purchasing refreshments and conversing with the ladies of the house.

Before long, Lenny became particularly interested in one of them and requested a loan of ten gold pieces.

Reluctantly Tilde granted his wish and also bestowed upon him the small blue potion which he had tried so vigorously to rid himself of.

Lenny was exceedingly grateful.

The mysterious little vial had finally found somebody who intended to use it for its advertised purpose.

Meanwhile, Tilde gradually pieced together the nature of the establishment's connection to the Thieves' Guild.

The women were not themselves thieves.

Their trade was information.

Men who visited Madame Butane's establishment were inclined to speak freely of their fortunes, their business dealings, their possessions, and their plans.

A merchant who had recently concluded a profitable venture might boast of his earnings. A traveler might mention when he intended to depart. A wealthy patron might reveal where he kept something of considerable value.

Such information could be sold.

And the Thieves' Guild was evidently an enthusiastic customer.

Madame Butane was not formally subordinate to Benny Hookhand, but their business relationship was sufficiently established that she could arrange an introduction.

Provided, naturally, that the arrangement was profitable.

Tilde produced his letter once more.

The madam recognized the author and appeared satisfied that introducing the elf was unlikely to jeopardize her own interests.

Her price was two hundred gold pieces.

For that sum, she could arrange a meeting before the night was over.

An arrangement was reached.

And so, while the rest of the adventurers slept beneath the silken sheets of Tywin Lannister's palace, Tilde waited in Madame Butane's establishment for an audience with the most notorious criminal in Ankh-Morpork.

Neither party knew precisely what the other was about to do.

## Part VIII: The Gnome with the Iron Hand

At two in the morning, Sigurd, Misty, Alikai, and their companions rose from their beds and proceeded toward the waterfront.

The city had grown quiet.

They divided themselves into two groups, taking up concealed positions from which they could observe the space between Docks Eight and Ten.

Alikai instructed his hawk to watch for new arrivals and return when something of interest occurred.

Then they waited.

There are few occupations less glorious than keeping watch in the darkness. The ground is uncomfortable, the hours are long, and every distant noise threatens to become interesting before invariably proving otherwise.

Eventually, the hawk returned.

Peering from their hiding places, the adventurers discerned three figures approaching the docks.

A well-dressed woman and two slender elves.

“Tilde?”

To their considerable surprise, they recognized their companion and Lenny in the company of Madame Butane.

Two waterfront ruffians greeted the newcomers. A brief conversation followed, after which the little gathering settled down to wait.

The concealed adventurers remained where they were.

Presently, a small, single-masted vessel emerged from the darkness upon the water.

It approached the unnumbered space and came alongside the stones.

From the vessel disembarked three dwarves, two burly humans, and a gnome.

The gnome possessed a hook in place of his hand.

Madame Butane stepped forward and introduced Tilde.

“So,” said Benny Hookhand, “there's been a great deal of mentioning my name around these parts.”

He knew why he was here, but he did not recognize the elf.

“What do you want?”

Satisfied that her obligation had been fulfilled, Madame Butane excused herself.

“I can see that you're getting along splendidly.”

And with that, she departed into the darkness.

Tilde explained his interest in establishing himself among the guild's ranks.

Benny seemed somewhat irritated that such a matter had warranted a personal audience. Nevertheless, if the elf wished to demonstrate his talents, there was a particular service he might perform.

Lord Tywin Lannister had become a source of considerable annoyance.

Within his palace, mounted upon the wall, was a treasured family heirloom.

A sword which, according to the legends of House Lannister, possessed the power to slay giants.

Benny desired it.

If Tilde could acquire the blade, he would receive a handsome payment and admission into the Thieves' Guild.

It was an interesting proposition, particularly for a man who was presently enjoying the count's hospitality.

From their concealed positions, however, the other adventurers could not hear the conversation clearly.

But with Madame Butane's departure they saw an opportunity which might not present itself again.

## Part IX: The Battle Before Dawn

Alikai called upon the ancient powers of the natural world.

Even among the paved stones of a city, life finds places to endure. Weeds and grasses grow between the cracks, taking root wherever the works of men have left them the smallest opportunity.

At the druid's command, that humble vegetation stirred.

The plants twisted and grew, entwining themselves around the legs of Benny's escort. Several of the criminals found themselves held fast against the stones, struggling vainly against the unnatural growth.

The adventurers emerged from concealment.

Alikai's animals rushed into the fray. His hawk descended upon the startled criminals while his badger and war dog attacked those upon the ground.

Misty and Babelzeru charged forward.

Crossbow bolts flew through the darkness.

One of Benny's human guards fell before he had properly understood what was happening.

Tilde, no less surprised than the men surrounding him, suddenly found his delicate negotiations interrupted by an eruption of violence.

Benny understood immediately.

“Treachery!”

The enchanted hook had apparently acquired a rather sound understanding of self-preservation.

Its gnomish bearer remained sufficiently free of Alikai's magic to retreat, and with little concern for his struggling associates, Benny leapt back aboard the vessel.

One of the dwarves attempted to cut his companions free, but the severed growth immediately began to renew itself.

Another dwarf received a most unfortunate command.

“Swim!”

Compelled by clerical magic, the dwarf leapt from the waterfront into the dark harbor.

Chainmail, though excellent protection against swords, possesses few qualities desirable in swimming attire.

The dwarf began to drown.

On the stones above, the second human guard was slain, and one of the dwarves fell beneath the attack of Alikai's dog.

Benny's vessel began to drift away.

But it carried more than its intended passenger.

Tilde had sprinted through the confusion and hurled himself aboard just as the boat departed the waterfront.

He now found himself alone with the master of the Thieves' Guild.

Alikai invoked his magic a second time.

The vessel was arrested before it could escape, and its occupants found themselves struggling against the same unnatural power that had ensnared the criminals upon the shore.

Benny was held fast.

Tilde, with the agility characteristic of his kind, managed to remain sufficiently free to fight.

With Benny held fast by the druid's enchantment, Tilde could scarcely have asked for a more favorable opportunity.

"Well, It was nice knowing ya Benny" quipped the elf, swinging his weapon with all the confidence of a man who had already imagined how the story would be told.

He missed spectacularly.

Benny, despite being magically restrained, remained entirely unharmed and considerably more irritated.

Meanwhile, the fighting upon the waterfront drew to its conclusion. The remaining criminals were overcome, and the adventurers turned their attention toward the little vessel.

Sigurd leapt aboard.

The warrior joined Tilde in subduing the gnome, and in the ensuing struggle, the hand bearing the enchanted hook was severed.

The gnome collapsed unconscious.

The fighting ceased.

There, upon the floor of the boat, lay Benny Hookhand.

A simple piece of metal, resting beside the bloodied body of its former bearer.

And not one of the victorious adventurers was particularly eager to pick it up.

## Part X: The Hand That Must Not Be Held

The company had recovered the object which Tywin Lannister desired.

They had also acquired a rather serious problem.

Nobody knew precisely how the enchantment worked. Whether possession required wearing the hook or merely touching it remained uncertain.

The difference was of considerable importance to anyone contemplating the retrieval of their prize.

As for the unconscious gnome, Misty and the others determined that his life ought to be spared.

Whatever crimes had been committed through his body, it was impossible to know how many had truly been his own.

Babelzeru knelt beside him and invoked a blessing of healing.

The wound was scarcely a minor one, but the priest's intervention was sufficient to preserve the unfortunate creature's life.

The gnome was bound and taken into custody.

The company gathered the belongings of the fallen criminals, leaving the accounting and division of their spoils for another occasion.

By four in the morning, they had returned to the palace.

The guards received their prisoner and conveyed him to the dungeons, where he would await questioning.

Tywin Lannister was unavailable at such an hour.

The count would have to wait until morning to learn what had transpired beneath the darkness of his own harbor.

And much remained to be settled.

The precise nature of Benny's enchantment had yet to be understood. The hook itself still presented a danger. The full extent of the guild's activities remained unknown, as did the question of what its members might do upon discovering the fate of their master.

There was also the curious matter of the Lannister family sword.

Had Benny coveted it merely as a valuable treasure, or did he have some particular purpose for a blade reputed to slay giants?

That, too, remained unanswered.

Thus ended a rather peculiar day in Ankh-Morpork.

The company had visited a dressmaker who made no dresses, searched for a dock which did not exist, escorted a distracted noblewoman, guided a procession of singing pilgrims, and negotiated unsuccessfully for magical merchandise.

Two of their number had narrowly escaped being devoured by a tiger.

And before the following sunrise, they had struck down the escort of the city's most notorious criminal and separated him from the body through which he ruled.

Misty had sought to restore order. Sigurd had finally found the battle he desired. Alikai had demonstrated that even the weeds of a city might serve the powers of nature.

And Tilde, after spending the greater part of the day attempting to secure an introduction to the Thieves' Guild, had scarcely concluded his first conversation with its master before his companions descended upon them with weapons drawn.

There are, admittedly, more conventional ways to end a business meeting.

Yet beneath the absurdities of the adventure lay something rather more troubling.

A criminal could be imprisoned. A corrupt guard could be dismissed. A stolen cargo could be recovered.

But what justice could be administered to a thing which possessed neither flesh nor blood, which commanded the bodies of others, and whose victims might themselves become the instruments of its crimes?

The adventurers had taken Benny Hookhand's hand.

Whether they had truly defeated Benny Hookhand was another matter entirely.
